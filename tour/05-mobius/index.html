From the disc to the half-plane

The Poincaré disc is not the only picture of hyperbolic geometry. With one clever map, the whole disc unrolls into the upper half of the plane, and that is where modular forms live.

The recap

In Lessons 3 and 4 we built hyperbolic geometry inside a disc: lines are arcs meeting the edge at right angles, and distance stretches so that the edge is infinitely far away. Now we want to move that whole geometry somewhere else without breaking it, the way a map of the world can be redrawn on a different projection.

Möbius transformations

The tool is a family of maps studied by August Ferdinand Möbius (1790–1868): w = (az + b) / (cz + d), where a, b, c and d are complex numbers with ad ≠ bc. Every one of them is built from three simple moves: sliding the plane along, turning and stretching it, and the move w = 1/z, which turns the plane inside out. They have three precious properties. They take circles and lines to circles and lines. They keep angles the same. And they keep the cross ratio of four points the same, which, as we saw in Lesson 4, means they keep hyperbolic distance the same.

Unrolling the disc

One particular Möbius map, a Cayley transform, does something remarkable: w = i(1 − z) / (1 + z) carries the inside of the disc exactly onto the upper half of the plane. The centre of the disc goes to the point i. The edge of the disc is unrolled onto the real line, with the single point −1 sent off to infinity. And because the map keeps circles, angles and distances, the lines of the disc become lines of the new picture: semicircles standing upright on the real line, or vertical half-lines.

Nothing about the geometry has changed. Two points are exactly as far apart after the move as before. The companion page shows the distance computed in each picture, side by side, and they always agree.

Why move at all?

As the thesis puts it, modular forms customarily "live" in the upper half-plane. The reason is that the symmetries we need next are beautifully simple there. In Lesson 6 we meet just two moves, z → z + 1 and z → −1/z, and both are Möbius maps of the half-plane onto itself.

For the mathematicians

The thesis (Chapter 2.3) shows that a linear fractional transformation maps the upper half-plane onto the unit disc, and the real line together with ∞ onto the unit circle, if and only if it has the form f(z) = eiα(z − z₀)/(z − z₀) with Im z₀ > 0 and α real. The page uses the inverse of the case α = π, z₀ = i, namely w = i(1 − z)/(1 + z), which sends −i, 1, i to −1, 0, 1.

Pulling back the disc's element of arc length 2|dz|/(1 − |z|²) gives ds = |dz| / y on the half-plane, and the thesis shows this does not depend on which Cayley transform is used. The page checks invariance numerically: it computes the disc distance with the thesis's formula and the half-plane distance with the standard equivalent, cosh d = 1 + |w₁ − w₂|² / (2 Im w₁ Im w₂).

Something to think about

In the half-plane, the circles of hyperbolic radius 1, 2 and 3 around i are ordinary circles, but their centres are not at i. Where are their centres, and why do they drift upwards as they grow?
