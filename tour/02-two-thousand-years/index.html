From Euclid to Ramanujan · Lesson 2 of 18

2,000 years of trying to prove it

For two thousand years, some of the best mathematicians in the world tried to show that Euclid's fifth postulate was unnecessary. They all failed, and the way they failed turned out to be the discovery.

The recap

In Lesson 1 we met Euclid's fifth postulate: if a line crosses two others and the inside angles on one side add up to less than 180°, those two lines meet on that side. It sounds obvious, but it is long and awkward next to Euclid's other four starting assumptions, and Euclid himself put off using it for as long as he could.

So mathematicians tried to prove it from the other four, which would have turned it from an assumption into a theorem. Ptolemy tried in the second century. Proclus, in the fifth century, pointed out Ptolemy's mistake and tried again. In the Islamic world Ibn al-Haytham, Omar Khayyam and Nasir al-Din al-Tusi took up the challenge, followed in Europe by Wallis, Saccheri, Lambert and Legendre. By 1767 d'Alembert was calling it the scandal of elementary geometry.

The trap they kept falling into

Almost every failed proof made the same mistake: somewhere along the way it quietly assumed something that sounds harmless but is really the fifth postulate in disguise. "Parallel lines stay the same distance apart." "You can scale a triangle up without changing its shape." "The angles of a triangle add to 180°." Each of these turns out to be equivalent to the fifth postulate, so using one to prove it is going round in a circle.

Saccheri, in 1733, tried something bolder. He assumed the postulate was false and hunted for a contradiction. He proved a long list of strange results, and then, because he was sure Euclid must be right, declared that one of them was absurd. He had in fact been doing non-Euclidean geometry without knowing it.

Letting it go

In the early 1800s three people, working separately, took the leap that Saccheri would not. By 1817 Gauss had become convinced the fifth postulate could not be proved, but he never published. Nikolai Lobachevsky in Kazan (1829) and János Bolyai in Hungary (1832) each published a complete geometry that keeps Euclid's first four postulates and throws away the fifth. In their geometry, through a point off a line there are many lines that never meet it, and the angles of a triangle add up to less than 180°.

That still left a worry. Perhaps the contradiction was there, and no one had found it yet. In 1868 Eugenio Beltrami settled it by building a working model of the new geometry entirely out of ordinary Euclidean pieces, and in 1871 Felix Klein recast it in a cleaner form. If the new geometry ever contradicted itself, so would Euclid's. After about two thousand years, the question was closed: the fifth postulate can never be proved from the other four.

For the mathematicians

Beltrami's and Klein's models give a relative consistency result: hyperbolic geometry is consistent if Euclidean geometry is. Since hyperbolic geometry satisfies Euclid's other axioms and denies Postulate V, Postulate V is independent of them. As the thesis puts it (Chapter 1), "it was finally determined that a proof of the postulate was impossible."

The Khayyam–Saccheri quadrilateral is the standard tool: two equal sides standing at right angles on a base. Its two top angles are equal, and the hypotheses of the right, acute and obtuse angle correspond to Euclidean, hyperbolic and elliptic geometry. Lambert noticed that under the acute-angle hypothesis a triangle's angle sum falls short of 180° by an amount that grows with its area. Klein's distance is built from Cayley's 1859 projective metric, using a cross ratio, an idea that returns in Lesson 4.

Something to think about

Gauss worked this out years before Lobachevsky and Bolyai, but kept quiet. Why might a mathematician sit on a discovery like this? And if a geometry has no contradictions in it, does that make it "true"?
