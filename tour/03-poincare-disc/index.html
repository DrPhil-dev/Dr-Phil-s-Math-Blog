The rules of the disc

From Euclid to Ramanujan · Lesson 3 of 18

Poincaré's disc: lines that are arcs

Lesson 2 ended with a promise: a geometry without the fifth postulate that is just as free of contradictions as Euclid's. Here is the most famous picture of it, and the one this whole tour is built on.

The recap

Draw a circle and call it the fundamental circle. Now agree on two rules. The points of our geometry are the points strictly inside the circle. The lines are the arcs of circles that cut the fundamental circle at right angles, together with its diameters. In the thesis these are called nominal points and nominal lines, to keep them apart from ordinary ones.

That is all. Everything else follows. Through any two points there is exactly one such arc, so two points still fix a line, just as in Euclid. Two of these lines can cross at most once. Angles between them are measured as ordinary angles between their tangents, so the disc shows angles honestly.

Where Euclid breaks

Pick a line and a point off it. In Euclid's geometry there is exactly one parallel through that point. In the disc there are infinitely many lines through the point that never meet the first line. Two of them are the boundary cases: they head for the very ends of the first line and only "meet" it on the edge, which is not part of the plane.

Triangles change too. Their sides bow inwards, and their angles always add up to less than 180°. The bigger the triangle, the bigger the shortfall. Try it on the companion page: drag the three corners towards the edge and watch the angle sum fall towards zero.

Why the edge is so far away

The disc looks finite, yet the geometry inside it has no edge you can reach. That is because distance is not measured with a ruler. Near the edge, every step counts for more and more, so a line that looks short on the page is infinitely long. That is the subject of Lesson 4. You can already see it in M. C. Escher's woodcuts. Escher used the Poincaré model for his four Circle Limit prints, after he was inspired by a figure in an article by the Canadian mathematician H. S. M. Coxeter. The shapes in those prints shrink on paper as they near the edge, which is how the disc shows distance growing there. (We link to the prints rather than copy them, as they are still in copyright.)

For the mathematicians

The thesis (Chapter 1.2) follows Siegel, Wolfe and Carslaw. Two nominal points P, Q determine a unique nominal line: the circle through P, Q and the inverse point P* = P/|P|² is the only circle through P and Q orthogonal to the fundamental circle, with the diameter as the degenerate case when P, Q and the centre are collinear (Siegel, Vol. II, p. 17). This page draws its lines exactly this way.

Reflection in a nominal line is inversion in the circle it lies on; displacements are compositions of these, and they preserve angle and nominal length (Wolfe, pp. 208–212). With these, every theorem of hyperbolic geometry has its counterpart in the disc, giving relative consistency. Historically, both Poincaré models were first found by Beltrami in 1868; Poincaré rediscovered them in 1882 and connected them with Fuchsian groups and automorphic functions, which is exactly the road this tour follows towards modular forms.

Something to think about

In the "A triangle" view, can you make the angle sum as small as you like? What would a triangle with all three corners on the edge look like, and what would its angles be?
