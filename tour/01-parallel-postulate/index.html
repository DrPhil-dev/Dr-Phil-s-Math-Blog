From Euclid to Ramanujan · Lesson 1 of 18

Two lines that never meet

My master's thesis ends with a strange sequence of numbers that Ramanujan found in 1916. To get there, we start about 2,300 years earlier, with Euclid and two straight lines.

The recap

Around 300 BC Euclid wrote the Elements, probably the most reproduced and studied book in the Western world after the Bible, with more than a thousand editions since printing began. He built geometry from five starting assumptions, his postulates. The first four are close to obvious: you can draw a straight line between any two points, extend it as far as you like, draw a circle with any centre and radius, and all right angles are equal.

The fifth is different. It is longer, it talks about what happens "if produced indefinitely", and for two thousand years mathematicians suspected it was not a true starting point at all, but something that ought to follow from the other four. They were wrong, and finding out why they were wrong opened the door to a new geometry. That geometry is where the rest of this tour takes place.

Euclid's own words

"That, if a straight line falling on two straight lines make the interior angles on the same side less than two right angles, the two straight lines, if produced indefinitely, meet on that side on which are the angles less than the two right angles."

In the diagram above: the "straight line falling on two straight lines" is the dashed cross-line, the "interior angles on the same side" are the two angles on the right, and "two right angles" is 180°. Turn the lower line and watch the lines meet on the side where the angles add up to less than 180°.

The everyday picture: parallel lines

When the two inside angles are both exactly 90°, neither side is "less than two right angles", and the lines never meet. Slide the cross-line as far as you like: it still meets both lines at right angles, and the gap is the same everywhere. This is the picture we all carry in our heads of parallel lines, like railway lines that stay the same distance apart forever.

For the mathematicians

Postulate V is one of several statements that turn out to be equivalent, given Euclid's other axioms. The best known is Playfair's axiom: through a point not on a given line there is exactly one line that does not meet it. Another is that the angles of every triangle add up to two right angles. Many of the failed "proofs" of Postulate V quietly assumed one of these equivalents, for example that parallel lines stay the same distance apart.

The thesis (Chapter 1) follows the story from there: Gauss, Lobachevsky (1829) and Bolyai (1832) worked out geometries that keep the first four postulates and deny the fifth, and Beltrami and Klein later built models of that geometry inside Euclidean geometry, showing it is consistent if Euclid's is.

Something to think about

Railway lines look as if they meet at the horizon, but they don't. Can you think of a surface on which "straight" lines really do meet, however carefully you draw them?
