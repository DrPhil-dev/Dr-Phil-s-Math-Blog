A butterfly that never gets smaller

The butterfly flies in a straight line of the disc's geometry, and every so often it leaves a footprint: an exact copy of itself. On your screen the copies shrink towards the rim, but measured the way Lesson 4 taught us, every one of them is exactly the same size, and each footprint is the same distance from the last. The butterfly keeps walking and never reaches the edge, because the edge is infinitely far away.

Notice that the far copies are not just smaller: they are squashed and bent, and the wings nearer the rim are smaller than the wings nearer the centre. Each copy is made by a Möbius transformation (Lesson 5), which keeps every angle the same but stretches different parts by different amounts. The faint background is a tiling of the disc by eight-sided tiles, three meeting at every corner, all of them the same size too.

A nod to Escher

M. C. Escher made the same idea famous with fish in his Circle Limit woodcuts. Those prints are still in copyright, so the butterfly here is an original drawing, made for this page.

For the mathematicians

Each copy is the image of one fixed butterfly near 0 under z ↦ Mc(Ta(eiφz)), where Ta(z) = (z + a)/(1 + āz) with a = tanh(s/2)eiφ, s the hyperbolic distance walked and Mc = Tc an optional offset of the path. The map is computed on the graphics card, vertex by vertex, so the butterfly is genuinely transformed, not just scaled. Its size on screen relative to the centre is 1 − |z|², the reciprocal of the factor in ds = 2|dz|/(1 − |z|²). Footprints are 0.8 units apart. The tiling is {8,3}: the central octagon has circumradius √(cos(π/8 + π/3) / cos(π/8 − π/3)), and the rest come from reflecting it in its sides (inversion in the sides' circles).
