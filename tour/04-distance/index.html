How far is the edge? Infinitely far

The Poincaré disc looks small enough to hold in your hand, yet you could walk inside it forever and never reach the rim. The secret is in how we measure distance.

The recap

In Lesson 3 the points of our geometry were the points inside a circle, and the lines were arcs meeting the edge at right angles. But a geometry needs more than points and lines: it needs a way to say how far apart two points are. A ruler will not do, because a ruler would make the disc finite, and we know the hyperbolic plane goes on forever.

So distance in the disc is stretched, more and more the closer you get to the edge. Start at the centre and take steps of exactly one unit each. The first step carries you almost halfway to the rim (about 46%). The next carries you less far on the page, and the next less again. After ten steps you are 99.99% of the way across, and the rim is still infinitely far away.

Measuring with four points

How do you actually measure? Take your two points, draw the one line through them, and follow it out to where it meets the edge, at two more points. Four points on one circle give a number called the cross ratio, which compares how far your two points are from each end. The distance between your points is the logarithm of that number.

The cross ratio has a magic property: any of the symmetries of the disc (the Möbius transformations of Lesson 5) leave it unchanged. So distances do not change when you move things around, which is exactly what a sensible distance should do. And as one of your points nears the edge, the cross ratio grows without limit, and so does its logarithm. That is why the edge is infinitely far away.

What "straight" means

On the companion page, measure two points along the arc, and then along the straight chord between them. The chord is longer. Because distance is cheaper near the centre, the shortest route bows inwards towards it. That is why the lines of this geometry are arcs: each one is the shortest path between its points. Which path is "straight" depends on how you measure.

For the mathematicians

Following Siegel, the thesis (Chapter 2.2) uses the cross ratio D(z₁, z₂, z₃, z₄) = ((z₁ − z₃)/(z₁ − z₄)) / ((z₂ − z₃)/(z₂ − z₄)), which is invariant under Möbius maps and is real exactly when the four points are concyclic (Appendix A.1). With z₃, z₄ the ends of the nominal line through z₁, z₂, in cyclic order, the distance is δ(z₁, z₂) = log D ≥ 0. It is symmetric, Möbius-invariant and satisfies the triangle inequality (Appendix A.2).

Moving z₁ to 0 and z₂ to a real t > 0 gives δ(0, t) = log((1 + t)/(1 − t)), and hence the closed form δ(z₁, z₂) = log((|1 − z₁z₂| + |z₂ − z₁|) / (|1 − z₁z₂| − |z₂ − z₁|)), with element of arc length ds = 2|dz| / (1 − |z|²). The page computes the distance both ways, by the cross ratio and by this formula, and the chord's length by integrating ds along it. A walker at distance d from the centre sits at radius tanh(d/2). The idea of measuring distance with a cross ratio goes back to Cayley's 1859 projective metric, which Klein used in 1871.

Something to think about

If every circle in the "Same size" view really is the same size, how many of them could you fit around the centre circle, touching it? In ordinary geometry the answer is six. Here, is it more or fewer?
