The theorem

For any three circles with distinct radii, the three external centers of similitude — the points where a common external tangent line pair for each ordered pair of circles converge — always lie on one straight line, the axis of similitude. Each internal center of similitude also lies on a line with two external centers, showing the four-line concurrency structure behind Monge's theorem.

A little history

The theorem is named for Gaspard Monge (1746 to 1818), a French mathematician usually credited as the founder of descriptive geometry, the technique of representing three-dimensional objects and solving spatial problems using two-dimensional projections (Britannica). He grew up in Beaune and showed an early talent for drawing and geometric construction, which eventually brought him to the military engineering school at Mézières.

It was there, while working out gun emplacements for a fortress design, that Monge devised a fast geometric method in place of the long arithmetic calculations normally used. The French army classified the method as a military secret, and Monge was not allowed to teach it openly for many years afterward. That work eventually grew into the general method of descriptive geometry (Linda Hall Library). That same background in geometric construction and projection is very much the kind of thinking behind this theorem: three circles, drawn in a plane, turn out to hide a straight line running through them.

Monge went on to have a broader career than just this one result. He helped found the École Polytechnique in Paris, taught and mentored a generation of French mathematicians and engineers, and joined Napoleon's expedition to Egypt, where he became the first president of the Institut d'Égypte in Cairo (Wikipedia). His name is one of seventy-two scientists and engineers engraved on the Eiffel Tower (Linda Hall Library).

The theorem itself is a small, elegant piece of that larger body of work. It is not one of Monge's most famous results, more of a geometric curiosity that carries his name because it grew out of the projective and constructive style of geometry he championed. It has since become a favourite example in classical geometry for showing how similitude, tangency, and collinearity connect in ways that are not obvious just from staring at three circles.
