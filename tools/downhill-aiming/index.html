A first mathematical model

Downhill Aiming

Why the popular "just use the horizontal distance" rule of thumb is close, but not exact, and what the correct aim-angle correction looks like once you go back to first principles.

1. The problem

A shooter at the origin sights a target along a line of sight of length \(D\), inclined at angle \(\alpha\) below the horizontal (downhill shots) or above it (uphill shots, \(\alpha < 0\) in this convention). Naively aiming straight down the line of sight overshoots on a downhill shot and undershoots on an uphill one, because gravity only acts on the bullet's true horizontal range, not on the slant distance to the target.

The widely used field fix is the horizontal-distance rule: ignore the slope, and hold as if the target were at the horizontal distance

\[ R_{\text{rule}} = D \cos\alpha \]

This is a good approximation but not exact, because it does not account for how the bore angle itself changes the shape of the trajectory. The question this piece answers: what is the exact aim-angle correction under a simple vacuum projectile model, and how far off is the rule of thumb in practice?

2. Setting up the geometry

Resolve the line of sight into horizontal and vertical components:

\[ R = D\cos\alpha, \qquad H = D\sin\alpha \]

where \(R\) is the horizontal range to the target and \(H\) is the vertical drop (or rise) to the target, both in the same length units. The shooter fires with muzzle velocity \(v\) at some launch angle \(\theta\) measured from horizontal, and we want the value of \(\theta\) that lands the bullet exactly on the target \((R, H)\).

3. Vacuum trajectory

Under gravity alone (no drag), the bullet's position at time \(t\) is

\[ x(t) = v\cos\theta \, t, \qquad y(t) = v\sin\theta \, t + \tfrac{1}{2} g t^2 \]

with \(y\) measured positive downward to match the downhill sign convention. Eliminating \(t = x / (v\cos\theta)\) and requiring \(y(R) = H\) gives

\[ H = R\tan\theta + \frac{gR^2}{2v^2\cos^2\theta} \]

Using \(\sec^2\theta = 1 + \tan^2\theta\), this becomes a quadratic in \(\tan\theta\):

\[ \frac{gR^2}{2v^2}\tan^2\theta - R\tan\theta + \left(\frac{gR^2}{2v^2} + H\right) = 0 \]

4. Solving for the launch angle

Applying the quadratic formula and taking the physically relevant (lower, flatter-trajectory) root gives the required launch angle:

\[ \tan\theta = \frac{\sqrt{v^4 - g^2R^2 + 2gv^2H} \; - \; v^2}{gR} \]

The quantity under the square root must stay non-negative for a real solution to exist; if it goes negative, the target is beyond what the muzzle velocity can reach along that line of sight at all.

The hold correction, how far above (or below) the line of sight the shooter needs to aim, is simply the difference between the line-of-sight angle and the required launch angle:

\[ \beta \;=\; \alpha - \theta \;=\; \alpha - \arctan\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{v^4 - g^2R^2 + 2gv^2H} \, - \, v^2}{gR}\right) \]

This is the exact vacuum-model answer: aim \(\beta\) above the line of sight (for a downhill shot with \(\alpha > 0\)) rather than holding dead-on at the horizontal-distance rule's flat range.

5. Checking it against a real rangefinder

The Leupold RX‑1300i, a laser rangefinder with a "True Ballistic Range" (TBR) mode, computes its own slope-adjusted hold distance using a fuller exterior-ballistics model that includes drag. It is a good real-world check on how close the vacuum model gets.

Example: a .270 Winchester, 130-grain bullet at a muzzle velocity of 930 m/s, ranged at \(D = 366\) m on a \(30°\) downhill slope.

MethodEquivalent hold range
Horizontal-distance rule (\(D\cos\alpha\))316.7 m
Ideal vacuum trajectory (this model)317.1 m
Leupold RX-1300i TBR (drag included)335.6 m

The vacuum model and the horizontal-distance rule agree with each other to within half a metre here, both landing well short of the drag-inclusive TBR figure. That gap between vacuum and TBR is exactly the air resistance the vacuum model leaves out, not an error in the geometry. The angle correction \(\beta\) derived above is the right shape of the fix; a full ballistic solver adds drag on top of it.
